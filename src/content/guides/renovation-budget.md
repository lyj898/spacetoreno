A renovation’s price is the sum of decisions. You can’t control the market, but you can control most of the decisions, and you can make sure nothing important is left out of the budget.

## What drives the cost

**How much comes out.** Hacking walls, floors and old carpentry costs money twice: once to remove, and again to make good. Laying new floor finishes over existing ones (allowed in HDB flats within 13mm including adhesive, with no permit) avoids much of that, where it suits the floor.

**Wet works.** Screeding, waterproofing, tiling and plastering are labour-heavy. Bathrooms and kitchens concentrate them, which is why those two rooms usually dominate a budget.

**Carpentry.** Custom cabinets and wardrobes are priced by size and by material. How much you build in is one of the biggest choices you control.

**Materials and fittings.** The same tile can come in a range of grades, and taps, lights and sanitary ware run from basic to premium. Specify them in the quote so you compare the same thing.

**Services.** Moving a gas point, adding power points or relocating a toilet each brings in a licensed trade and, in HDB flats, sometimes a permit.

**The home itself.** A resale flat usually has more to replace than a new one. Older homes may need rewiring, and while MOM says buildings built after 1 January 1991 are unlikely to contain asbestos, older ones may, and only an approved contractor may remove it.

**Professional fees.** Structural changes need a Professional Engineer, and some landed-house works need a Qualified Person to submit plans. In HDB flats, taking down a non-load-bearing concrete wall needs a PE to supervise.

## The costs outside the contract

Easy to forget, and worth a line each in your budget:

- debris removal and disposal, if it isn’t in the quote;
- protection of common areas, and any condo renovation deposit;
- appliances, lights, curtains and loose furniture;
- air-conditioning;
- a post-renovation clean;
- moving out, storage or somewhere to stay while the works go on: see [living elsewhere during a renovation](/during-the-works/living-elsewhere-during-renovation/);
- your time, for site visits and decisions.

## Keep a contingency

Some problems only show once hacking starts: uneven floors, old pipes, wiring that needs replacing. And most people change something along the way. Keep a contingency you don’t plan to spend, and make sure your contract says how variations are priced. The consumer regulator’s renovation guide says variations should only be made with your express agreement.

## Paying for it

Pay in stages tied to the work, never far ahead: see [how renovation payments are staged](/hiring/renovation-payment-schedule/). If you’re considering a bank renovation loan, MAS rules let banks treat it as one only if it’s repaid within 5 years and your renovation loans from that bank total no more than 6 months’ income or S$30,000, whichever is lower.

## Size the budget to the claims limit, too

CASE noted in 2025 that home renovation contracts today commonly exceed S$20,000, which is the usual limit of the Small Claims Tribunals. That doesn’t change what you should spend, but it’s one more reason to pay in stages and choose a firm with deposit protection.
